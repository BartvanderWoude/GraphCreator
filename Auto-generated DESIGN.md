# Typed DAGs fill epidemiology's tooling gap

## General

Causal DAG editors already exist, and one of them is well established. **DAGitty** is the field's standard. It is browser-based, easy to learn, and offers the widest set of analyses. Its web interface, however, has had no feature release since **v3.1 in July 2023**. Most newer tools (shinyDAG, DAGDraw, ggdag) are R or Shiny wrappers around its engine. None of the tools surveyed lets you annotate nodes as categorical, ordinal or numerical, and none exports a typed model you could simulate from. That is your clearest opening. A typed schema maps almost directly onto simDAG and DoWhy-GCM simulation code, and from there onto the synthetic-SCM priors used for tabular foundation models. Built-in support for the reporting checklist Tennant et al. derived from 234 health papers would add a second advantage, and so would clean round-tripping to dagitty, DOT, GML and node-link JSON. Do the drawing and dagitty export well first, so epidemiologists can adopt it. Then compete on the typed model. For the build, a **React 19 + TypeScript + Vite** single-page app is the low-risk choice. Use **React Flow (@xyflow/react, MIT)** for the canvas, Zustand for state, Zod for the file schema, the `yaml` package for I/O and shadcn/ui on Tailwind for components. No backend is needed. Drive Claude Code from a written spec with hard pass/fail checks. Host the static build free on **GitHub Pages or Cloudflare**, with graphs kept in the browser. That design largely avoids GDPR exposure and is a selling point for researchers whose study designs are unpublished. One decision you must make on purpose is licensing. DAGitty's JavaScript algorithm library is GPL, so bundling it makes your app copyleft. Writing the algorithms yourself keeps MIT open.

## DAGitty still sets the bar, and most rivals wrap it

The incumbent is mature but static. DAGitty's web interface was last reimplemented in v3.0 (2020), with touch support, and **v3.1 (2023-07-11)** added selection variables. The site moved servers in October 2023, and no newer release notes appear ([dagitty.net](https://www.dagitty.net/)). Its manual describes a strong analytic core. You can mark exposure, outcome, adjusted, latent and selected nodes with keyboard shortcuts. It computes minimal sufficient adjustment sets, testable conditional independencies and instruments, and it colours causal paths green and biasing paths red. Export covers PDF, SVG, JPEG and PNG plus its own dot-like model syntax ([DAGitty manual 3.x](https://dagitty.net:443/manual-3.x.pdf)). Sharing is weaker. Published DAGs are **public**, anonymous posts cannot be edited later, the offline version cannot export raster images, and the manual itself warns there is "no guarantee that dagitty.net will run indefinitely" ([DAGitty manual](https://dagitty.net:443/manual-3.x.pdf)). The R package is still maintained: its last commit, in October 2026, was a dependency security fix, not new features ([r-universe](https://jtextor.r-universe.dev/api/packages/dagitty)).

The alternatives mostly wrap that engine or serve one narrow need. **shinyDAG** exports LaTeX, PDF and ggdag/dagitty objects, but it has 52 stars and has not been updated since December 2019 ([ecosyste.ms](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2FGerkeLab%2FshinyDAG)). **DAGDraw** (an ISPOR 2025 poster) is a Shiny app built on dagitty, ggdag and DiagrammeR. Its authors built it because of the "poor or outdated interfaces and installation requirements of existing tools", and they admit usability testing is still pending ([ISPOR](https://www.ispor.org/heor-resources/presentations-database/presentation-cti/ispor-2025/poster-session-3/dagdraw-a-responsive-and-modern-directed-acyclic-graph-drawing-tool-for-causal-inference-and-transportability-analysis)). Causal Wizard is a browser editor built on cytoscape.js and DoWhy that also estimates effects from uploaded data. Its own author says it lacks some of DAGitty's functions, and the review is a vendor self-assessment ([Medium](https://medium.com/@causalwizard/online-causal-diagram-and-dag-drawing-editing-tools-900bb1815c86)). Tetrad is powerful but needs a local Java install. GeNIe is Windows-only and paid. BARD requires applying for an account (same source).

Usability evidence comes mainly from a systematic comparison of TikZ, DAGitty, ggdag, dagR and igraph. Pitts and Fowler (2024) found the DAGitty website **drag-and-click and quick to learn**, with the widest range of analyses. Its R plots, however, have thin, faint lines and no subscripts. ggdag took **about twice as long** for the same figure and needed "multiple Google searches". dagR codes edges as number pairs, which the authors rated "least intuitive". Their conclusion: no single tool covers drawing, analysis, simulation and publication figures together ([Pitts & Fowler, arXiv 2305.12006](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.12006)). The answer to "how easy are they?" is therefore mixed. DAGitty is easy for quick sketches but looks dated, and the result is not publication-grade. The R tools give good figures at a real time cost, and everything else either needs installation or is barely maintained.

The new activity is in **LLM-assisted construction**. DAGForge (arXiv, July 2026) is a browser-based system. It builds a frozen PubMed snapshot, makes LLM pairwise causal judgments grounded in verbatim excerpts, and attaches confidence, rationale, PMIDs and a provenance tag to each edge. It reaches a directed F1 of about 0.71 against literature DAGs ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.21859.md)). EpiCausalX (medRxiv, Feb 2026) does a similar job with LangGraph and NetworkX, but its usability test had only 12 participants and it reports no accuracy check ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.06.26345723v1.full)). The caution comes from Aziz and Brookhart (2025). In their tests, GPT-4o and o1 **omitted race from every one of 15 statin–CVD DAGs**, and up to 4 of 10 cited sources did not exist. They concluded that LLMs "cannot replace a domain expert's judgment" ([Clin Epidemiol 2025](https://www.dovepress.com/can-contemporary-large-language-models-provide-the-domain-knowledge-ne-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-CLEP)). The space has users and is still moving, but nobody holds the "modern, well-maintained, interoperable manual editor" niche.

## Typed variables and simulatable export are the unclaimed gap

Your planned feature of annotating variable types is, on the evidence, the most distinctive one. DAGitty's documented model has roles and positions but **no variable-type attributes and no edge attributes** ([DAGitty manual](https://www.dagitty.net/manual-3.x.pdf)). Among drawing tools, only dagR touches types, by simulating mixed binary and continuous data, and its interface is the weakest of the group ([Pitts & Fowler](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.12006)). No standard for causal-graph variable-type metadata turned up in dagitty, DoWhy, networkx, bnlearn or Tetrad, so your own YAML schema is justified rather than reinventing something that already exists.

A typed node becomes valuable once it drives code generation. R's **simDAG** defines nodes as `node(name, type, parents, formula, betas, intercept)`. Root types include `rbernoulli` and `rcategorical`, and child types include `gaussian`, `binomial`, `multinomial`, `poisson` and `cox` ([simDAG docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/simDAG/html/node.html)). **DoWhy's GCM** module assigns one mechanism per node by data type. It uses additive-noise models for continuous variables, discrete ANMs for discrete ones, and classifier-based mechanisms for categorical variables, which "should be represented as strings" ([DoWhy GCM](https://www.pywhy.org/dowhy/main/user_guide/modeling_gcm/graphical_causal_model_types.html)). If a node carries type (categorical/ordinal/numerical), levels or ordering, and optionally a distribution, and an edge carries a coefficient or functional form, both scripts follow almost mechanically. None of the surveyed GUI tools does this. This also connects to your own research. A typed DAG with functional forms is one hand-specified member of a prior over SCMs. A "sample N datasets from this DAG with randomised edge strengths and nonlinearities" button would be new for epidemiologists. It would also be directly useful to anyone building or stress-testing tabular foundation models on realistic medical structures. No source documents this link yet, so it is an inference, but a well-founded one.

The second gap is **reporting discipline**. Tennant et al. reviewed 234 applied health papers and found that only **21% stated an estimand** and **49% reported the DAG-implied adjustment set**. **73%** reported estimates from other or unclear adjustment sets, and **no article tested its DAG against its data**. Extraction errors affected 39% of DAGs and were lower for those drawn in DAGitty (32% vs 50%) ([Tennant et al., IJE 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/180527/1/dyaa213.pdf)). Their eight recommendations can mostly be enforced in software:

| Tennant recommendation                | Feature that enforces it                                          |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| State focal relationship and estimand | Required estimand field, exposure/outcome mandatory before export |
| Make DAG available                    | Persistent, versioned, citable export (YAML + dagitty + SVG/TikZ) |
| Include unobserved confounders        | First-class latent nodes, prompt "any unmeasured common causes?"  |
| Arcs flow in one direction            | Auto-layout left-to-right, warning on back-flowing arcs           |
| Assume arcs, justify omissions        | "Missing arc" justification list for node pairs without arcs      |
| State and use implied adjustment set  | Auto-computed adjustment sets in an exportable reporting summary  |
| Justify alternative sets              | Named alternative sets with free-text rationale                   |

A third gap is **auditable evidence per edge**. Ferguson et al.'s ESC-DAGs protocol assesses each edge for temporality, face validity and theory. It records every keep, reverse or delete decision in a log, as transparency "in lieu of objectivity" ([Ferguson et al., IJE 2020](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/49/1/322/5536330)). An edge with optional fields for citations, rationale and decision history serves that workflow without needing an LLM. If you later add LLM suggestions, the Aziz–Brookhart and DAGForge results argue for making them opt-in, labelled by provenance, and citation-verified. DAGForge is now the closest competitor on that axis, so treat it as an extension rather than your core pitch.

**Interoperability** decides whether anyone adopts the tool. Dagitty syntax (`dag { X [exposure] Y [outcome] X -> Y }`, with `pos="x,y"`, `latent`, `adjusted`, `<->` and grouped edges) is the field's de facto exchange format ([DAGitty manual](https://www.dagitty.net/manual-3.x.pdf)). The R package's `convert()` already bridges it to TikZ, lavaan, bnlearn and causaleffect ([rdocumentation](https://rdocumentation.org/packages/dagitty/versions/0.3-4/topics/convert)). The formats differ in how much of your metadata they can carry:

| Format                   | Ecosystem                                                                                                                                                          | Carries node attributes (your types)?                                     | Priority                                         |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Own YAML (+ JSON Schema) | Your tool                                                                                                                                                          | Full: roles, types, levels, evidence, estimand                            | Source of truth                                  |
| dagitty syntax           | DAGitty, dagitty-R, ggdag                                                                                                                                          | Roles + positions only ([manual](https://www.dagitty.net/manual-3.x.pdf)) | Import + export, essential                       |
| GML                      | DoWhy (recommended input) ([DoWhy](https://petergtz.github.io/dowhy/main/example_notebooks/load_graph_example.html))                                               | Yes                                                                       | Export                                           |
| DOT                      | DoWhy, Graphviz                                                                                                                                                    | Yes, via `[key=value]`                                                    | Import + export                                  |
| node-link JSON           | networkx, Python generally ([networkx](https://networkx.org/documentation/stable/reference/readwrite/generated/networkx.readwrite.json_graph.node_link_data.html)) | Yes                                                                       | Export (write `edges`, accept `links` on import) |
| bnlearn modelstring      | bnlearn ([bnlearn](https://www.bnlearn.com/examples/dag))                                                                                                          | No, structure only                                                        | Export                                           |
| Tetrad text              | Tetrad, causal-learn ([Tetrad manual](https://tetrad-manual.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/graph.formats.md.txt))                                               | Latent flag only                                                          | Export, later                                    |

The newer R package caugi already writes DOT, Mermaid, GraphML and JSON. Its comparison table shows dagitty and pcalg with no such support ([caugi vignette](https://frederikfabriciusbjerre.r-universe.dev/caugi/doc/comparisons.Rmd)). That confirms the gap, and it gives you a good test partner. A minimal schema could look like this. Version it explicitly so files stay readable as the format evolves:

```yaml
schema: causal-dag/0.1
estimand: { exposure: smoking, outcome: lbw, type: total_effect }
nodes:
  - id: smoking
    label: Maternal smoking
    role: exposure # exposure|outcome|latent|adjusted|selected|other
    var_type: categorical # categorical|ordinal|numerical
    levels: [never, former, current]
    pos: [0, 0]
  - id: ses
    role: other
    var_type: ordinal
    levels: [low, mid, high] # order is meaningful
edges:
  - { from: ses, to: smoking, evidence: ["PMID:123"], note: "..." }
omitted_arcs:
  - { between: [ses, lbw], justification: "..." }
```

## React, TypeScript and React Flow minimise risk for a non-frontend developer

Pick React rather than Svelte or Vue mainly because of how much documentation exists for each, not because React is technically better. **@xyflow/react 12.12.1** (React Flow, MIT, "and it will be forever") is the leading node-editor library. Its free examples include _Custom Nodes_, _Preventing Cycles_, _Validation_, _Save and Restore_, _Dagre Tree_, _Elkjs Tree_, _Context Menu_ and _Download Image_. These cover most of an MVP, and Claude Code can adapt them ([React Flow examples](https://reactflow.dev/examples), [React Flow Pro](https://reactflow.dev/pro)). Svelte Flow exists from the same team, but it has fewer examples and templates, and Svelte 5's rune syntax is recent enough that a coding agent makes more API mistakes with it. Skip Next.js. A purely client-side editor needs no server rendering or API routes, and Vite produces static files any host can serve ([npm vite](https://www.npmjs.com/package/vite)). The paid Pro tier ($169/month and up) gates only example code such as Auto Layout and Undo/Redo, not library features ([React Flow Pro](https://reactflow.dev/pro)). You can rebuild both from free parts.

The recommended stack, with versions current on npm as of 6 Nov 2026:

| Concern             | Choice                                              | Why                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Build / language    | Vite 8 + React 19 + TypeScript                      | Static output, fastest scaffold ([npm react](https://www.npmjs.com/package/react))                                                                      |
| Canvas              | @xyflow/react 12                                    | Nodes are React components; `isValidConnection` hook for cycle checks ([examples](https://reactflow.dev/examples))                                      |
| State               | Zustand 5                                           | React Flow uses it internally and its state guide is written for it ([state mgmt](https://reactflow.dev/learn/advanced-use/state-management))           |

| Persistence         | Zustand `persist` to localStorage                   | DAGs are a few KB; IndexedDB (idb-keyval) only if needed                                                                                                |
| Tests               | Vitest (logic) + Playwright (end-to-end)            | Both double as Claude's pass/fail checks                                                                                                                |

Plan two architectural points early. First, **keep all graph logic in pure TypeScript modules with no React imports**: serialisation, cycle checks, d-separation, adjustment sets, exporters. Test them with Vitest. That is the part of the codebase you can reason about as a programmer, and you can cross-check it against dagitty-R outputs on a gallery of known DAGs. The UI can stay a thinner layer you review less closely. Second, **decide the licence before writing analysis code**. DAGitty's `jslib` implements adjustment sets and implied independencies in JavaScript and "could also be used independently". It is licensed **GPL-2.0 or any later version** and is not on npm ([dagitty GitHub](https://github.com/jtextor/dagitty), [DAGitty manual](https://www.dagitty.net/manual-3.x.pdf)). Vendoring it makes your distributed app GPL. That suits an open academic tool, but it closes off permissive reuse. Reading and writing dagitty's _text format_ triggers no copyleft. If you want MIT, write d-separation (Bayes-Ball) and back-door adjustment-set enumeration yourself. They are well-specified, testable algorithms, and Claude Code handles them well when given reference outputs. Note that elkjs is EPL-2.0/GPL-3.0 dual-licensed ([npm elkjs](https://www.npmjs.com/package/elkjs)), which is another reason to start with dagre.

Stay fully client-side until a feature truly needs Python. If one does, for example fitting mechanisms to uploaded data or reusing DoWhy, **Pyodide** in a module-type Web Worker keeps the app static ([Pyodide worker docs](https://pyodide.org/en/stable/usage/webworker.html)). The cost is a multi-megabyte first load. A FastAPI backend gives you full CPython, but it adds hosting, CORS handling, a second codebase and, once accounts exist, GDPR obligations.

## Run Claude Code from a spec, with hard checks and one feature per session

Anthropic's own guidance names the failure modes: missing information, no way for Claude to check its work, and rules that must never be broken. The remedy is to "tell Claude what it can't see, give it a check to run, and enforce the rules that can't have exceptions" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). For this project that comes down to a short routine:

**Scaffold and specify.** Run `npm create vite@latest dag-builder -- --template react-ts` (or `pnpm dlx shadcn@latest init -t vite`), then `git init`. Before any feature work, prompt Claude with "Interview me until you could write the spec, then write it to SPEC.md". The questions should cover node roles, variable types, edge rules, the YAML schema, the export list and what is out of scope. Then start a fresh session to build from the spec ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Run `/init` and trim CLAUDE.md to under 200 lines. Keep only the exact dev, build, typecheck, test and single-test commands, plus decisions Claude cannot infer from the repo: "Zod schema in `src/model` is the source of truth", "graph logic has no React imports", "no backend" (same source).

**Make checks mechanical.** Add a Stop hook so Claude cannot finish while typecheck fails, for example `"command": "pnpm tsc --noEmit 1>&2 || exit 2"` in `.claude/settings.json`, and a PostToolUse hook that runs Prettier on edited files. The guidance is explicit that a CLAUDE.md line Claude keeps ignoring belongs in a hook ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Treat Vitest round-trip tests as your definition of done: YAML to model to YAML, and model to dagitty to model. For visual checks, Claude in Chrome (`claude --chrome`) can open `localhost:5173`, click through flows, read console errors and take screenshots. It needs a Pro, Max, Team or Enterprise plan and does not work in WSL ([Claude Code + Chrome](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/chrome)). Playwright MCP is the alternative, and its README notes that the Playwright CLI with skills is more token-efficient for coding agents ([playwright-mcp](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright-mcp)). Committed Playwright tests remain the durable check.

**Feed current docs.** Several of these libraries changed APIs across major versions: the package moved from `reactflow` to `@xyflow/react`, and Zod went from 3 to 4 and Tailwind from 3 to 4. Model training data mixes the old and new forms. **Context7** supplies version-specific documentation to Claude via `npx ctx7 setup --claude` ([Context7](https://github.com/upstash/context7)). The `frontend-design` skill counters the generic "SaaS-card" look by asking for a small token system (4–6 colours, typefaces, layout sketch) up front ([frontend-design SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anthropics/skills/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)). For a tool aimed at publication figures, that matters more than it seems.

**Build in thin slices.** Use one feature per session, each ending with tests passing and a git commit, and `/clear` between tasks. After two failed corrections, `/clear` and rewrite the prompt instead of arguing ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). A sensible order: (1) canvas with add/delete/rename nodes, (2) edges with cycle prevention, (3) roles and variable-type panel, (4) YAML import/export with Zod validation, (5) dagitty import/export, (6) localStorage persistence and undo/redo (snapshot on drag-stop, not every pixel), (7) dagre auto-layout, (8) SVG/TikZ export, (9) adjustment sets and a Tennant reporting panel, (10) simDAG/DoWhy code generation. Ask for "be ambitious" when you want empty and error states handled, because otherwise Claude builds the smallest thing that satisfies the request. Use `/code-review` or a read-only reviewer subagent against SPEC.md before merging larger slices (same source).

Start with GitHub Actions running typecheck, Vitest and Playwright, then deploy to GitHub Pages or Cloudflare. **Put a custom domain on it from day one**, such as a university subdomain or a cheap `.org`/`.eu`. URLs printed in papers then survive a change of host, which is exactly the longevity weakness the DAGitty manual admits. Graphs stored in localStorage or downloaded files mean the app itself processes no patient or personal data, and DAGs encode assumptions, not records. What remains is the host's access logs, any third-party fonts or scripts (self-host fonts), and analytics. Plausible uses no cookies or persistent identifiers, hashes IPs with a daily-rotated salt, and stores data in the EU ([Plausible](https://plausible.io/data-policy)). CNIL treats only narrowly configured audience measurement as consent-exempt, and it names Matomo as configurable that way ([CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/en/sheet-ndeg16-use-analytics-your-websites-and-applications)). Use one of those, or no analytics at all, and you avoid a cookie banner. Share graphs by **compressing the model into the URL fragment**. Fragments are never sent to the server, so you get DAGitty-style short links without its public model store. If you later need hosted saving or collaboration, Supabase's free tier (500 MB database, 50,000 MAU) works, but free projects **pause after a week of inactivity** ([Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing.md)). Adding accounts turns you into a data controller with a privacy policy and processor agreements to manage. Present the tool as research methodology, not clinical decision support, so the question of medical-device regulation never arises.

## Make it citable and validated from the first commit

Academic tools spread through citations, so build the scholarly pieces in early. Add a **CITATION.cff** and connect the repo to Zenodo, which issues a DOI for each GitHub release ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/archiving-a-github-repository/referencing-and-citing-content), [Zenodo](https://help.zenodo.org/docs/github/)). If you want a JOSS paper, make the repo **public early**. JOSS requires more than **six months of public development history**, an OSI licence, **evidence of real research use** rather than intended use, tests, documentation, and an AI-usage disclosure ([JOSS docs](https://joss.readthedocs.io/en/latest/submitting.html)). That makes JOSS a post-beta target. A methods-journal software paper in the line of DAGitty's IJE papers ([Lübeck record](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/de/publications/robust-causal-inference-using-directed-acyclic-graphs-the-r-packa/)) could run in parallel. A typed-SCM or synthetic-data angle gives such a paper something DAGitty cannot claim.

The other development practices below are general good practice, not drawn from cited sources here, but they matter for this audience:

- **Small companion packages.** An R package that reads your YAML into dagitty or ggdag objects and a Python package that loads it into networkx or DoWhy. Epidemiologists live in R, and this removes the "poor compatibility with statistical software" complaint DAGDraw's authors raised.
- **An example gallery.** Canonical structures such as M-bias, colliders, mediation and confounding by indication, with citations. These double as regression fixtures checked against dagitty-R outputs.
- **A keyboard-operable list view of nodes and edges.** Canvases are hard for screen readers, and colour should not be the only cue for roles or path types.
- **A versioned JSON Schema** for the YAML format.
- **Dependabot.**
- **Think-aloud sessions with five to eight epidemiologists.** Start before the analysis features, because DAGDraw's untested usability is a gap you can close.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the project. A "nicer DAGitty" alone would be a thin contribution, because DAGitty is still easy enough and the other wrappers have failed to displace it. The defensible product treats the DAG as a **typed, documented causal model rather than a picture**. Variable types, levels and edge evidence go in. Tennant-compliant reports, dagitty and DoWhy-ready files, and runnable simulation code come out. That positioning also gives you a research identity. Your work on synthetic SCM priors gives you an unusual position to make "draw a DAG, get realistic synthetic data" credible, and few epidemiology tool-builders have that background.

Three decisions shape everything downstream and should be settled in SPEC.md before Claude writes analysis code. The first is the licence: GPL with vendored dagitty, or MIT with your own algorithms. The second is the YAML schema, versioned from 0.1. The third is a firm "no backend" boundary for the first release. Get dagitty round-tripping right before anything clever, so epidemiologists can adopt the tool. The typed-export features are what will keep them using it.
